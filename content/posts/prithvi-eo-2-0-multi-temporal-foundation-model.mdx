Prithvi-EO-2.0 is an Earth-observation foundation model with 300M and 600M ViT backbones, pretrained with masked autoencoding on NASA’s global Harmonized Landsat–Sentinel archive. Each pretraining sample contains four 256 × 256 frames at 30 m across six HLS bands. Optional time-and-location embeddings define the TL variants; the authors report an 8% aggregate GEO-Bench gain over Prithvi-EO-1.0.

<KeyTakeaways>

- The paper's pretraining samples contain four dates; 3D positional encodings span time, height, and width, while the convolutional temporal patch size is one.
- `-TL` checkpoints add learned location and date biases to image tokens; plain checkpoints are useful controls.
- The Hub lists six checkpoints across four parameter counts—not a plain/TL pair at every size.
- The 8% headline is an aggregate. Task-level metrics, data splits, and the model variant matter when deciding what it means for your work.

</KeyTakeaways>

## What does Prithvi-EO-2.0 actually add?

The backbone is a Vision Transformer trained as a masked autoencoder. The paper changes both the patch representation and positional encoding: each frame is divided into patches with a 3D convolution whose temporal patch size is `t = 1`, then time, height, and width receive combined 3D sin/cos positional encodings. Time is therefore represented across the sequence of image tokens; this is not a convolution that mixes four months into one temporal patch.

In the paper's like-for-like comparison, the new 300M and 600M backbones are paired with a much larger global corpus than the US-only pretraining used for the 100M predecessor. That scale-up is part of the result; it is not an architecture-only comparison.

The second change is metadata. A temporal-location model receives each image's acquisition date plus the sample's center latitude and longitude. The model encodes these separately and adds them to embedded tokens through learned weighted sums. During pretraining, the authors set metadata dropout to 0.1 so the model also learns to run when location, date, or both are missing. Metadata acts as a learned bias; it is not another spectral band.

## What exactly went into pretraining?

The sampling design is as important as the architecture: four observations span months, not four adjacent days. The authors sampled sequences from HLS tiles over a decade, then filtered for clouds, missing pixels, and spatial overlap between training and validation areas.

| Pretraining detail | Reported setup |
| --- | --- |
| Source product | NASA/USGS Harmonized Landsat and Sentinel-2 (HLS) version 2 |
| Coverage and cadence | 2014–2023; four timestamps per sample, 1–6 months apart |
| Spectral input | Six common reflectance bands: blue, green, red, narrow NIR, SWIR 1, SWIR 2 |
| Sample geometry | Four 256 × 256 frames; random crop to 4 × 224 × 224 for pretraining |
| Dataset split | 4.2M training patches and 46K validation patches |
| Objective and schedule | Masked autoencoder; 400 epochs |
| Pretraining hardware | 300M: 80 A100 GPUs, about 21K GPU-hours; 600M: 240 A100 GPUs, about 58K GPU-hours |

Source: the authors' [data sampling and model-architecture sections](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.02732#S3.SS1) and [model card](https://huggingface.co/ibm-nasa-geospatial/Prithvi-EO-2.0-600M-TL). The corpus is global, but the exact sample list is not bundled with the downloadable weights.

## Does the GEO-Bench gain survive a closer read?

The evaluation uses [GEO-Bench](https://github.com/ServiceNow/geo-bench): six classification and six semantic-segmentation datasets, ten hyperparameter trials per dataset, and ten repeated runs with different seeds. The paper reports that 600M-TL outperforms Prithvi-EO-1.0 by 8% across the benchmark and also beats six other geospatial foundation models across tasks with input resolutions from 0.1 m to 15 m.

That is useful evidence, but the abstract does not say whether the 8% is a mean, median, or another aggregation. It combines task families with different metrics, so it cannot establish that every dataset improves by 8%, or that the metadata embeddings caused the full gain. The paper's task tables are more actionable than the headline: they let you inspect a metric, test set, and checkpoint at a time.

### What about ecosystem monitoring?

In a separate gross-primary-productivity experiment, the authors used 37 globally distributed flux towers (975 samples) and left one year out for testing. Prithvi-EO-2.0-600M-TL reported R² values of **0.88, 0.80, 0.83, and 0.74** for held-out 2018, 2019, 2020, and 2021, respectively; the paper reports an average of 0.81, compared with 0.75 for ResNet18. This is a distinct ecosystem-dynamics task, not another GEO-Bench score ([paper Table XV](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.02732#S4.T15)).

## Where do the metadata embeddings help, and where could they hurt?

Optional metadata is a good engineering choice. Real pipelines lose acquisition dates when scenes are mosaicked and lose coordinates when tiles are shuffled into training shards. A model that treats both as hints is more tolerant of missing fields than one that requires them.

The same design has a failure mode worth naming. Location and day-of-year are strong seasonality and geography priors. A model given those features can learn “this texture looks like July in Iowa” and get crop classification right for reasons that do not transfer to a new continent, a shifted growing season, or an anomalous year. The published evaluations are broad, but they are still evaluations on datasets the consortium helped assemble, with subject-matter experts in the loop. That collaboration is a strength for relevance and a limitation for independence: end-user-driven benchmarks tend to test the conditions the end users care about.

The test is cheap, which is why I would run it first. Fine-tune the `-TL` and plain checkpoints on the same split, then evaluate both on a held-out region and a held-out year. If the metadata version wins only in-distribution, you have measured a prior rather than a representation.

## How would I reproduce the fine-tuning?

You can reproduce the fine-tunes more directly than the pretraining. The paper states that all of its fine-tuning experiments use a single GPU, and the [NASA-IMPACT repository](https://github.com/NASA-IMPACT/Prithvi-EO-2.0) ships dataset configs and examples through [TerraTorch](https://github.com/IBM/terratorch). That does not make pretraining a one-GPU job: the 300M and 600M runs used 80 and 240 A100s, respectively.

```python
from terratorch.registry import BACKBONE_REGISTRY

model = BACKBONE_REGISTRY.build("prithvi_eo_v2_600_tl", pretrained=True)
```

My order of operations, from cheapest to most informative:

1. **Sen1Floods11.** Small, public, binary, and fast enough to run several times. It checks band handling and normalization before a larger science question enters the picture.
2. **HLS burn scars.** Multi-temporal and close to the pretraining distribution; the project repository includes a fine-tuning example.
3. **Multi-temporal crop segmentation.** The task where date embeddings should matter most, so it is a good place for a matched TL/plain ablation.
4. **BioMassters or Landslide4Sense.** These test regression or a different sensor geometry, rather than only one segmentation setup.

Fix the split before running anything. A benchmark gain is meaningless if the fine-tuning split drifts between runs. Also pin the TerraTorch version instead of tracking `main`; configuration keys can move.

## What do the authors emphasize?

The architecture section distinguishes token position inside an image from the time-and-location context supplied to the model. These are the authors' words, quoted directly and attributed to the paper's full author group:

> “Note that the metadata encodings are designed to inform the model about the sample’s general location (center coordinates) and corresponding timestamp, while only the 3D positional encodings are responsible for conveying the positions of the tokens within the sample.”
>
> — Szwarcman et al., [model-architecture section (III-B)](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.02732#S3.SS2)

The abstract also makes a project-level claim:

> “The project exemplifies the Trusted Open Science approach embraced by all involved organizations.”
>
> — Szwarcman et al., [paper abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.02732)

The first distinction clarifies that geolocation and acquisition dates are context, not spatial token coordinates. The second describes process, not independent validation: the released configs, exact splits, and repeatable evaluation are the evidence to inspect.

## What would I check before pointing it at my own data?

- **Band order and units.** Six HLS reflectance bands, in the model-card order: blue, green, red, narrow NIR, SWIR 1, SWIR 2.
- **Temporal ordering.** The four pretraining frames are chronological and separated by months. Keep inference scenes ordered and tied to the same location.
- **Metadata expectations.** The `-TL` variants accept coordinates and dates; plain checkpoints do not use those embeddings. Do not compare unmatched model sizes and call it a metadata ablation.
- **Licence split.** The code repository is MIT-licensed. Check the individual [weight card](https://huggingface.co/ibm-nasa-geospatial/Prithvi-EO-2.0-600M-TL) for the terms on the model artifact.
- **Reconstruction sanity check.** The card provides `inference.py` for masked-patch reconstruction. Run it on a known HLS scene to validate preprocessing before interpreting downstream predictions.

## What does this release get right?

The release is unusually testable: public checkpoints, fine-tuning configs, sample notebooks, and a benchmark protocol with repeated runs. The [architecture description](https://huggingface.co/ibm-nasa-geospatial/Prithvi-EO-2.0-600M-TL) is specific enough to inspect the 3D positional encoding, metadata bias, and dropout behavior, while the [paper's task tables](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.02732#S4.T4) let readers inspect scores beyond the abstract's aggregate.

The remaining gap is the pretraining corpus: the paper describes its sampling and filtering, but the exact 4.2M-patch sequence list is not distributed with the weights. You can test the released representation and reproduce fine-tuning more readily than rebuild the precise pretraining run.
