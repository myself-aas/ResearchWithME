A p-value describes how unusual a test statistic is under a specified null model and its assumptions. It is not the probability that a hypothesis is true, an effect-size estimate, or a deployment decision. Read it alongside the estimand, uncertainty interval, study design, and analysis choices rather than translating a threshold into certainty.

| Main limitation | Inverting a conditional probability or treating a threshold as practical importance |

This is not an argument against hypothesis tests. It is an argument for reading them precisely, because
the imprecise reading has a way of travelling further than the careful one.

## The four misreadings

| What people write | What the p-value means |
| --- | --- |
| "There is a 3% chance the null is true." | Under the null model and its assumptions, the chosen statistic is at least this extreme with probability 0.03. |
| "We have shown there is no effect." | A large p-value is a failure to detect, not evidence of absence. |
| "The effect is large because p is small." | p conflates effect size with sample size. |
| "p = 0.049 is significant; p = 0.051 is not." | The threshold is a convention; the underlying quantity is continuous. |

The first row is the most common and the most consequential. It inverts the conditional probability,
which is the kind of error that sounds technical until you notice it is exactly how most press
releases read.

## A concrete example

Consider illustrative summary values, not a real trial or a reconstruction from a specified sample: an estimated difference of 0.4 standard deviations and a reported p-value of 0.02. The design and test must be supplied before either can be checked. The interval below is also illustrative, not derived from an unstated sample size.

What can you say?

- You can say the data are unlikely under the null, at the conventional threshold.
- You can say the estimated effect is 0.4 SD, with a confidence interval that excludes zero.
- You cannot say the probability that the treatment works is 98%.
- You cannot say the effect is important. That is a question about consequences, not statistics.

<Callout tone="danger" title="The sentence to avoid">
  "The treatment is effective (p &lt; 0.05)." Write instead: "The estimated effect was 0.4 SD
  (95% CI 0.06–0.74); the data are unusual under the null model." One sentence is a claim about data;
  the other is a claim about the world that the test cannot support.
</Callout>

## Confidence intervals do more work

If I could change one habit in how statistical results are reported, it would be to lead with the
interval rather than the test. An interval carries the estimate, its precision, and the direction of
uncertainty in a single object. A p-value collapses all of that into one number whose meaning is easy
to state and hard to remember.

A useful reading question is how the analysis was selected. An unusual statistic under a stated null is not automatically strong evidence for the particular substantive explanation a reader prefers.

## What to do instead

1. Report the estimate and its interval, always, before the test.
2. State the model and the null explicitly. "No difference in means, assuming independent observations"
   is a claim you can check.
3. Pre-register the analysis, or label it exploratory. Both are honest; conflating them is not.
4. Treat the threshold as a convention, and say so when a result sits near it.

## Reproduction playbook

**Proposed evaluation.** No new experiment is reported by this editorial revision.

1. Write down the null model, the statistic and its tail rule before calculating a p-value. Record sampling, stopping, exclusions and multiplicity; when reviewing existing work, mark unspecified choices rather than assuming a clean preregistered analysis.

2. Use a labeled synthetic example or a documented dataset to compare estimates, intervals and test outputs. If you simulate repeated samples, retain the generator, seed, number of repetitions and model assumptions; those simulations do not validate an unrelated real study.

3. Report the effect in meaningful units with its uncertainty and decision consequences. Distinguish exploratory from prespecified analyses and explain how assumption failures would change interpretation rather than hiding behind a significance threshold.

## Pre-publication audit

- [ ] The p-value is stated conditionally on a null model and its assumptions.
- [ ] Effect size, uncertainty and practical consequences are reported separately.
- [ ] Toy values and illustrative figures are not presented as a real clinical trial.

## Verdict

Keep hypothesis tests in their proper role: conditional model checks that accompany effect estimates and design information. Neither a small p-value nor a wide interval decides whether an intervention matters. Better reporting makes the assumptions visible instead of asking one number to carry the whole argument.
