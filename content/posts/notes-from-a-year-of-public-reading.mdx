A year ago I moved my reading notes out of a private folder and into public posts. The change was
supposed to be about accountability. It turned out to be mostly about memory.

## The private notebook problem

Private notes have no reader, and notes without a reader drift toward shorthand. Six months later, a
line like "check the ablation — suspicious" is worse than useless: it records that I had a thought
without recording what the thought was.

Writing for a public reader forces the missing sentence. Not because the reader needs it, but because
the act of writing it is how I discover whether I actually have it.

<Callout tone="tip" title="The test I use">
  If a note cannot be understood six months later without the paper open beside it, it is not a note.
  It is a bookmark.
</Callout>

## What changed

Three things, in order of surprise:

1. **Fewer abandoned threads.** A thread I have written about is a thread I return to. Publication
   creates a small obligation that private notes never do.
2. **Slower conclusions.** Knowing someone might check the claim makes me look for the load-bearing
   assumption before I write the sentence. This has killed several conclusions I was fond of.
3. **A usable archive.** Search over my own writing now works better than search over my bookmarks,
   because the writing contains the reasoning rather than the URL.

## The parts that did not improve

Public writing also has costs worth naming. I second-guess hedged sentences more than I should. I have
caught myself softening a criticism because the author might read it — and then caught myself doing it
again. And there is a real temptation to publish the version of a finding that makes a cleaner post.

<Sidenote>
  My rule for the last one: if the interesting part of the result is the caveat, the post leads with
  the caveat. A tidy conclusion that requires the caveat to be true is not a conclusion.
</Sidenote>

## The practice, concretely

Nothing elaborate. One folder of Markdown files, one post when a thread reaches a conclusion, and a
short list of open questions carried between posts. The only unusual rule is that I do not publish a
post until I can state, in one paragraph, what I believed before reading and what I believe now. If the
two paragraphs are identical, there is no post yet.

That rule has saved me from publishing roughly a third of what I drafted. It has also made the
remaining two thirds better, which is the part I did not expect.
