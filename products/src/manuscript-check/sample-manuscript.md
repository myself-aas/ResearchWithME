# Spaced Retrieval Practice and Exam Performance: A Sample Manuscript

This file is a made-up manuscript for trying the tool. The study, its data, and its references do not exist. Several mistakes are planted on purpose so that each check has something to find.

## Abstract

Retrieval practice improves long-term retention, but its effect in large introductory courses is unclear. We randomly assigned 184 undergraduates to spaced retrieval practice or to rereading for six weeks. Students in the retrieval group scored significantly higher on the final exam (M = 74.2) than the rereading group (M = 68.9), and 81% of them passed compared with 66% of the rereading group. The effect was consistently larger for students with lower prior attainment. Spaced retrieval practice is an effective, low-cost addition to large courses.

## Introduction

Testing oneself on material strengthens memory more than studying it again (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). The benefit grows when practice is spread over time (Cepeda et al., 2008). Most of this evidence comes from laboratory studies with short delays, and classroom trials have produced mixed results (Morgan, 2015; Santos and Idris, 2019).

Large introductory courses are a hard case. Attendance varies, students differ widely in preparation, and instructors have little time for extra assessment. A randomised controlled trial (RCT) in this setting would show whether the laboratory benefit survives. Earlier work by Halloran (2017) suggested that it might not.

This study is the first to test spaced retrieval practice over a full teaching block in a course of this size. We show that the RCT design is feasible with existing LMS tools and that the gain is robust across levels of prior attainment.

## Methods

Participants were 184 first-year students enrolled in an introductory psychology course. They were randomly assigned by the LMS to a retrieval condition (n = 93) or a rereading condition (n = 91). The retrieval group completed three short quizzes a week on material from earlier weeks; the rereading group received the same material as summary pages. Figure 1 shows the schedule. Prior attainment was the mean of two entry tests.

The outcome was the score on the final exam, marked blind to condition. Groups were compared with independent-samples t tests, pass rates with a chi-square test, and the moderating role of prior attainment with linear regression. Table 2 gives the descriptive statistics.

## Results

The retrieval group scored higher on the final exam than the rereading group, t(182) = 2.31, p = .004. A higher share of the retrieval group passed, χ2(1) = 4.90, p = .027. Exam score was related to prior attainment, r(182) = .42, p < .001, and the interaction between condition and prior attainment was reliable, F(1, 180) = 5.12, p = .025. Quiz completion did not differ between the first and second half of the block, t(92) = 1.20, p = .233. Figure 3 plots the interaction.

## Discussion

Spaced retrieval practice raised exam scores in a large course, and the pass rate rose with it. The size of the gain is in line with classroom estimates reported by Morgan (2015) and larger than the estimate of Whitfield and Osei (2020). Because quizzes were delivered through the LMS, the cost to instructors was small. The study was limited to one course in one institution, and exam marks are an imperfect measure of learning. We did not measure retention after the course ended, we did not record how long students spent on the summary pages, we could not prevent students in the rereading condition from testing themselves, and although the entry tests were marked blind we cannot rule out that students in the retrieval condition simply spent more time on the material overall, which would mean that part of the effect we attribute to retrieval is an effect of time on task.
