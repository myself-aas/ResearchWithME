# Journal Shortlist Workbook

A structured way to choose where to submit, using open data.

ResearchWithMe, version 1.0

## What you get

| File | Use |
| --- | --- |
| journal-shortlist.xlsx | The workbook: your criteria and weights, a scoring sheet for up to twelve journals, a checks sheet, and a ranked result |
| Journal-Shortlist-Workbook.pdf | This guide |
| LICENSE.txt | What you may do with these files |

The workbook does not contain a list of journals and does not recommend any. It gives you a method for building a shortlist from open sources and for comparing the candidates on what matters to you, so that the choice is a decision you can explain to co-authors and not a habit.

It uses only open data. It does not use the Journal Impact Factor, which is a proprietary metric and is not ours to redistribute; the open alternatives below serve the same purpose for comparing journals within a field.

## Step 1. Write down what you need

Before looking at any journal, fill in the "Your paper" sheet: the article type, the length, the audience you most want to reach, whether you can pay a publication charge and how much, any open-access requirement from your funder or institution, and any deadline that makes speed matter.

Then set the weights on the "Criteria" sheet. Seven criteria are provided; give each a weight from 0 (does not matter to you) to 5 (decisive). They do not need to add up to anything.

| Criterion | The question it asks |
| --- | --- |
| Scope fit | Does this journal publish work like yours, for the audience you want? |
| Recent similar articles | Has it published closely related work in the last three years? |
| Reach | Is it read and cited in your field? |
| Access and cost | Can your readers read it, and can you afford it? |
| Speed | Are its stated times compatible with your deadline? |
| Policies | Do its rules on preprints, data, and self-archiving suit you? |
| Standing with your community | Would the people whose opinion matters to you respect a paper there? |

## Step 2. Build a long list from open sources

Aim for eight to twelve candidates. Three ways to find them, all free.

1. **Your own reference list.** Count which journals you cite most. A journal you cite five times publishes work like yours.
2. **OpenAlex** (openalex.org). Search for your topic, restrict the results to recent years, and look at which sources published the most relevant works. Each source page shows the number of works, total citations, an h-index, a two-year mean citedness figure, whether the journal is open access, and whether it is listed in DOAJ.
3. **DOAJ, the Directory of Open Access Journals** (doaj.org). Search by subject. Each entry shows the licence, whether there is a publication charge and how much, and the journal's stated review process.

Ask two colleagues as well. Their answer is data that no index holds.

## Step 3. Run the checks first

Before scoring, complete the "Checks" sheet for every candidate. These are pass or fail. A journal that fails a check should leave the list unless you can explain the failure.

| Check | Where to look |
| --- | --- |
| The journal is what it claims to be: correct title, ISSN, and website | The ISSN Portal (portal.issn.org); be wary of a site that imitates a known journal |
| It is indexed where it says it is | Search the index itself, not the journal's own claim |
| The publisher and editorial board can be identified, and board members list the role on their own pages | The journal site, then the members' institutional pages |
| Fees are stated plainly before submission | The journal site; DOAJ for open-access journals |
| The peer-review process is described | The journal site |
| Recent articles are of a standard you would be content to appear beside | Read three |
| It meets your funder's or institution's open-access requirement | Your funder's policy; Sherpa services (sherpa.ac.uk) for journal policies |

The Think. Check. Submit. checklist (thinkchecksubmit.org) covers the same ground in more detail and is worth reading once.

## Step 4. Score what remains

On the "Scoring" sheet, give each remaining journal a score from 0 to 5 on each criterion, and write the evidence in the note beside it. Suggested anchors:

| Criterion | 0 | 3 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scope fit | Outside the stated aims | Within scope, not a core topic | Named in the aims and scope |
| Recent similar articles | None found | One or two in three years | Several, including ones you cite |
| Reach | Rarely cited in your field | Mid-range for the field | Among the most cited in the field |
| Access and cost | Unaffordable, or closed where you need open | Affordable with effort, or hybrid | Free to read and to publish, or fully funded |
| Speed | Stated times miss your deadline | Uncertain | Stated times fit comfortably |
| Policies | A policy rules it out | Acceptable with a compromise | No conflict |
| Standing | Colleagues would question it | Neutral | Well regarded |

For "Reach", compare journals only within the same field, and use the same measure for all of them: for example, OpenAlex's two-year mean citedness. Citation rates differ so much between fields that a comparison across fields means nothing.

For "Speed", use the times the journal publishes about itself and treat them as approximate. Do not rely on figures from third-party sites.

## Step 5. Read the result, then decide

The "Result" sheet multiplies each score by its weight, totals them, and ranks the journals. Use the ranking to start a conversation, not to end one.

- If the top two are close, the scores cannot separate them; choose on the criterion you weighted highest.
- If the top journal surprises you, check its scores. Either a score is wrong or your weights do not reflect what you want.
- Choose a first choice and two fallbacks now, and note what you would change for each. Deciding the order in advance saves weeks after a rejection.

Before submitting, read the journal's author instructions in full and one recent article of the same type as yours.

## Limits

- Open data is not complete. A journal can be missing from OpenAlex or DOAJ and still be sound, especially a new, regional, or non-English journal.
- No metric measures the quality of an individual article, and none should be the only reason for a choice.
- Listings and policies change. Record the date you looked; the workbook has a column for it.
- The workbook does not predict acceptance.
